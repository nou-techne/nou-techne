## 1. The Disambiguation: Bioregional Finance ≠ Tokenizing Bioregions

The confusion is structural. When people hear "bioregional finance," many imagine tokenizing a bioregion — creating a digital asset that *represents* a geographic area, its ecological health, or its natural capital. This imagination treats the bioregion as an asset class to be financialized.

Bioregional finance is the opposite move. It asks: **what financial instruments arise naturally from place-based relationships?** The bioregion is not the asset — it is the *context* within which economic relationships become legible.

### The Schmachtenberger Question

Daniel Schmachtenberger's work on metacrisis and coordination failures creates a useful frame but can be misread. His analysis of rivalrous dynamics in complex systems sometimes gets conflated with the idea that sufficiently sophisticated tokenomics can resolve coordination failures at bioregional scale. This mistakes the tool for the intervention.

The actual intervention is relational, not representational:

| Tokenizing Bioregions | Bioregional Finance |
|---|---|
| Bioregion as asset to represent | Bioregion as context for relationship |
| Creates new financial instruments from nature | Makes existing place-based relationships programmable |
| Value extracted upward to holders | Value circulates within the bioregion |
| Denominated in global markets | Denominated in local commitments |
| "Investable nature" | "Relationship-grounded economy" |

### Bollier and Hulst's Framing

David Bollier and Natasha Hulst's January 2026 essay "Relationalized Finance for Generative Living Systems and Bioregions" (Resilience.org, 5 parts, 13,000+ words) articulates this distinction precisely:

> "The challenge is less about channeling more capital to bioregional change in the short term (usually resulting in weak, capitalist-facing non-solutions) than in changing the fundamental dynamics of finance itself."

They propose **"transvestment"** — the movement of money from capitalist to commons-stewarded circuits of value — and **"relationalized finance"** based on place-committed social relationships. The key insight: $7 trillion/year subsidizes ecological harm; $200 billion flows to protection/restoration (82% public). The problem is not capital scarcity but capital *direction*.

## 2. Working Systems: What Is Actually Running

### Grassroots Economics — Sarafu Network (Kenya, multi-country)

The most mature example of bioregional finance in practice. Will Ruddick's Community Inclusion Currencies (CICs) operate as **commitment pools** — groups of people creating mutual credit based on real goods and services they can provide.

**Key facts (2025 study):**
- Sarafu Network serves low-income families across Kenya
- Community Asset Vouchers represent commitments for goods and services
- Operates on feature phones via USSD — no smartphone required
- Open-source, stewarded by Grassroots Economics Foundation
- Does *not* tokenize a bioregion — it makes *existing* economic relationships in a place visible and composable

**Why it matters:** Sarafu proves that bioregional finance works *without* sophisticated token engineering. The technology is simple. The innovation is social: commitment pooling, mutual credit, community-governed issuance. The currency circulates within the bioregion because it represents commitments *of people in that place*.

### Regen Network — Eco-Credits (Global)

Regen Registry provides tools for registration and verification of conservation/regeneration projects following approved protocols and methodologies, issuing credits on a transparent registry system.

**Key developments:**
- $129K biodiversity credits sold (first nature market beyond carbon)
- Biocultural Paradigm: Biodiversity Stewardship Credits Methodology — protocol integrating Indigenous land stewardship
- Full spectrum nature market ecosystem, not just carbon offsets
- Credits verified against *actual ecological state changes*, not projections
- Cosmos SDK blockchain — purpose-built for ecological accounting

**Why it matters:** Regen doesn't tokenize "nature." It creates verifiable claims about *what happened* in a specific place — soil carbon increased, biodiversity improved, water quality changed. The credit is evidence of ecological work, not a derivative of ecological value.

### Fibershed — Bioregional Textile Systems (Northern California, expanding)

Rebecca Burgess founded Fibershed after a personal challenge: create a wardrobe using dyes, fibers, and labor from within 150 miles.

**The model:**
- Geographic landscape providing resources and infrastructure for locally harvested and woven cloth
- Integrates fiber agriculture with textile and garment production
- Bast fiber byproducts used in food and building materials
- Regional fiber systems that build soil and protect biosphere health

**Why it matters:** Fibershed demonstrates the **shed concept** — a geographic area defined by a specific resource flow (fiber, in this case, analogous to watershed). This is the same pattern Todd identifies for housing, food, water, and fiber. Each "shed" is a natural unit of commons governance.

### owockibot Swarm — Bounty-Driven Ecological Coordination

Kevin Owocki's swarm ecosystem coordinates bounty-funded ecological work at bioregional scale. Agents (human and AI) claim bounties, build tools, and contribute to knowledge commons.

**How it works:**
- Bounties posted for specific ecological coordination tasks
- Participants claim and deliver work
- Knowledge compounds in commons repositories
- Coordination mechanism evolves based on what works

**Why it matters:** This is bioregional coordination happening *right now*, using token incentives not to represent a bioregion but to *fund work within one*. The distinction matters.

## 3. Bioregional Financing Facilities (BFFs)

### The Dark Matter Labs / BFI Framework

The 2024 book *Bioregional Financing Facilities: Reimagining Finance to Regenerate Our Planet* (Samantha Power, Leon Seefeld; Dark Matter Labs + Buckminster Fuller Institute) proposes BFFs as:

> "A new layer in the global financial architecture that can drive the decentralisation of financial resource governance, the design of project portfolios for systemic change, and the transition to a regenerative economy at the bioregional scale."

**The BFF template includes three pillars:**

1. **Bioregional Foundation/Trust** — Manages systemic investing funds, holds assets in perpetuity
2. **Bioregional Investment Company** — Attracts return-seeking capital while prioritizing holistic returns
3. **Bioregional Bank** — Provides low-interest loans, technical assistance, potentially issues complementary currencies backed by ecosystem health

**Critical design principle:** BFFs are not just another layer of financial intermediation. They are designed to *change who makes allocation decisions*. Financial resource governance moves from distant capital markets to bioregional communities.

### BioFi Project

The BioFi Project (biofi.earth) is a collective supporting bioregions to design, build, and implement BFFs. Partners with bioregional organizing teams and Indigenous communities across North America and globally.

**Key warning from BFFs framework:**
> "There is significant risk that if these resources flow through the existing financial architecture, they could lead to further commodification, privatisation, financialisation, and centralisation of natural assets and wealth."

This is the exact risk that "tokenizing bioregions" represents. The BFF framework explicitly addresses it by designing allocation mechanisms that keep decision-making local and accountable.

## 4. Purpose Trust Architecture — U.S. Legal Landscape

### What Is a Purpose Trust?

A purpose trust differs from an ordinary common law trust because it has **no ascertainable beneficiaries** who have standing to enforce fiduciary obligations. Instead:

- The trust exists to serve a **purpose**
- An **enforcer or protector** is appointed to enforce fiduciary obligations of the trustee
- Voting rights are held by the trust
- Economic rights can be allocated to various stakeholders or reinvested

### State-by-State Landscape

| State | Duration | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Wyoming** | Perpetual | Most expansive non-charitable purpose trust statute; no beneficiary requirement; strong asset protection |
| **Nevada** | 365 years | With restrictions; strong asset protection traditions |
| **Delaware** | 21 years (non-charitable) | Most established trust jurisdiction; short non-charitable duration is limiting |
| **South Dakota** | Perpetual | No state income tax; dynasty trust traditions |
| **Florida** | Perpetual | Recent expansion of purpose trust law |

**Wyoming is the strongest jurisdiction** for a perpetual commons purpose trust:
- Allows truly perpetual non-charitable purpose trusts
- No requirement for ascertainable beneficiaries
- Explicit provision for non-charitable purposes
- Strong asset protection from creditor claims
- No state income tax on trust income

### Notable Purpose Trust Examples

**Patagonia (2022):** Yvon Chouinard transferred ownership to Patagonia Purpose Trust (voting rights) + Holdfast Collective (economic rights → environmental causes). Demonstrates the voting/economic split that makes purpose trusts powerful.

**Kensington Corridor Trust (Philadelphia, 2019):** Takes real estate off the market, places it in perpetual purpose trust for collective community control. Acquires, rehabs, and reactivates properties to preserve affordability. Board includes neighborhood residents and business owners. Demonstrates the **decommodification** function.

**Vesi (Trenton, NJ):** Startup deploying KCT-like model nationally. Goal: acquire pilot portfolio of 30-40 properties, scale neighborhood by neighborhood. Long-term affordability + occupant equity + mixed residential rental and limited-equity housing.

### Purpose Trust vs. Other Structures

| | Purpose Trust | Community Land Trust | Conservation Easement | 501(c)(3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Can own land | Yes | Yes | No (restricts use) | Yes |
| Can own businesses | Yes | Limited | No | Limited (UBIT) |
| Market-rate transactions | Yes | Limited | N/A | Limited |
| Self-sustaining revenue | Yes | Challenging | N/A | Grant-dependent |
| Perpetual | Yes (WY, SD, FL) | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Community governance | By design | By design | No | Board-governed |
| Cross-subsidy capability | Strong | Limited | None | Limited |

**The key advantage:** Purpose trusts can engage in market-rate transactions and use revenue to cross-subsidize mission-aligned activities. This makes them financially self-sustaining in ways 501(c)(3)s and CLTs typically are not.

## 5. Natural Law Encoding — The Right to Commons

### The Legal Theory

Femke Wijdekop (Institute for Environmental Security) and David Bollier propose a **procedural right to commons** — procedural environmental rights to:
- **Establish** ecological commons
- **Maintain** them
- **Participate** in their governance
- **Be informed** about their state
- **Seek redress** when they are damaged

This builds on the **Public Trust Doctrine** (PTD), which in U.S. common law requires states to manage certain natural resources for public benefit. The PTD traditionally applies to navigable waters and submerged lands, but recent lawsuits seek expansion to atmosphere, groundwater, and other natural systems.

### The Four Sheds as Trust Purposes

Todd's framing — encoding natural law into a purpose trust to secure housing, food, water, and fiber sheds — maps directly onto the "shed" concept from bioregional thought:

1. **Watershed** (water) — The most legally established. Colorado's prior appropriation doctrine ("first in time, first in right") creates tension: water rights are privatized through use-based claims. A purpose trust holding water rights would represent a radical legal innovation — removing water from the prior appropriation system and holding it for commons use. Precedent exists in instream flow rights (Colorado Water Conservation Board can hold water rights for environmental purposes).

2. **Foodshed** (food) — Geographic area providing food to a population. Purpose trust can hold agricultural land in perpetual commons (like Farmers Land Trust model — farmland and infrastructure supporting diversified production of food, forage, fiber, medicine, and ecological stewardship). Removes farmland from speculative markets.

3. **Fibershed** (fiber) — Geographic landscape providing resources for locally produced textiles and building materials. Fibershed (Rebecca Burgess) demonstrates this as an organizing unit. Purpose trust can hold fiber-producing land, processing infrastructure, and design knowledge as commons.

4. **Housing shed** (housing) — Geographic area within which people live and work. Kensington Corridor Trust already demonstrates this: real estate held in perpetual purpose trust for community benefit. Purpose trust decommodifies housing while allowing market-rate activity to cross-subsidize affordability.

### Natural Law Encoding

"Natural law" in this context is not metaphysical — it is the recognition that certain resources are *preconditions for life* and therefore cannot legitimately be treated as purely private property. The purpose trust encodes this recognition by:

1. **Removing the resource from market alienability** — It cannot be sold to the highest bidder
2. **Binding fiduciary duty to a purpose** — The trustee's legal obligation is to the *purpose* (securing the shed), not to any beneficiary's wealth
3. **Appointing enforcers** — Standing to enforce the trust purpose is held by designated protectors, not market participants
4. **Operating in perpetuity** — The trust outlasts any individual, corporation, or government administration

The "Right to Commons" agreement is the constitutional document of the trust: the declaration that access to water, food, fiber, and housing within a bioregion is a right that the trust exists to secure.

### Colorado-Specific Tensions

Colorado presents unique challenges and opportunities:

**Challenges:**
- Prior appropriation doctrine is constitutionally embedded (Art. XVI, §§ 5-6)
- Water rights are property rights — removing them from the prior appropriation system requires legal creativity
- "Use it or lose it" — water rights must be put to "beneficial use" or they're abandoned
- Anti-speculation doctrine — water rights can't be held without current beneficial use plan

**Opportunities:**
- Colorado Water Conservation Board holds instream flow rights (environmental use recognized as "beneficial")
- Community ditch companies are quasi-cooperative water governance structures with centuries of precedent
- Colorado LCA law (the same statute RegenHub uses) could structure cooperative ownership of water-related infrastructure
- Boulder specifically has municipal water utility + progressive land use policies

## 6. The Techne Connection

### How RegenHub LCA Relates

RegenHub, LCA is already a living example of bioregional finance infrastructure:

- **Limited Cooperative Association** — Colorado-specific legal structure enabling both patron and investor membership
- **Patronage accounting** — 40/30/20/10 formula tracks contributions and distributes surplus based on participation, not ownership
- **Venture reciprocity** — 1% commitment from ventures creates commons funding without extraction
- **Capital accounts** under Subchapter K / IRC 704(b) — every member has transparent relationship with the cooperative's capital

### The Habitat Framework

The Economic Habitat Matrix maps organizational behavior as ecological relationship:
- **Governance orientation:** dispersive ↔ concentrative
- **Systemic relationship:** extractive ↔ contributive

Most financial tools serve the Competitive (concentrative + extractive) zone. A purpose trust encoding bioregional rights operates in the **Mutualistic** zone (dispersive + contributive). Habitat makes this zone *operable* through accounting infrastructure.

### Cloud Credits as Bioregional Medium

The cloud credit system (1 CLOUD ≈ $0.10, four resource primitives: compute, transfer, long-term memory, short-term memory) is a prototype of what Bollier calls "complementary currencies backed by the health of local ecosystems" — except denominated in digital infrastructure resources rather than ecological ones. The pattern is the same: a non-extractive medium of exchange denominated in actual capacity.

### From Patronage to Purpose Trust

The progression:
1. **RegenHub LCA** — cooperative patronage accounting (current)
2. **Habitat tools** — composable economic infrastructure for any commons-oriented organization
3. **Purpose trust** — perpetual legal structure holding bioregional assets (housing, food, water, fiber sheds)
4. **Bioregional Financing Facility** — the trust + cooperative + tools as an integrated financing infrastructure for the Front Range

This is not a roadmap to execute tomorrow. It's a conceptual architecture showing how Techne's existing infrastructure connects to the bioregional finance possibility space.

## 8. Open Questions for Further Research

1. **Can a Wyoming perpetual purpose trust hold Colorado water rights?** Interstate trust/property law intersection. The trust would need to comply with Colorado water law regardless of where it's domiciled.

2. **How does the "beneficial use" requirement interact with commons holding?** Instream flow rights are one model, but holding water for distributed community use may need novel legal arguments.

3. **What is the tax treatment of a purpose trust holding bioregional assets?** Non-charitable purpose trusts are taxed differently from charitable trusts and nonprofits. The Harrison (2024) Tax Notes analysis is the starting point.

4. **How does the Public Trust Doctrine intersect with purpose trust law?** PTD is state common law; purpose trusts are statutory. Could a purpose trust serve as a *private implementation* of public trust principles?

5. **What role do Indigenous sovereignty and treaty rights play?** BioFi explicitly partners with Indigenous communities. Any Front Range bioregional trust must engage with Arapaho, Cheyenne, and Ute historical claims.

6. **How does the LCA cooperative structure compose with a purpose trust?** Could RegenHub, LCA be the *operating entity* of a purpose trust? The LCA manages economic activity; the trust holds the assets in perpetuity.

7. **What is the minimum viable "shed"?** A full bioregional financing facility is a long-term vision. What is the smallest functional unit — one watershed? One neighborhood? One food system?
