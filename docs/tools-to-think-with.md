## A Note to the Reader

This document assumes no prior knowledge of computers or mathematics. It assumes only that you are a human being who has ever tried to remember something important, share something with someone far away, or make a decision more complex than your own mind could hold at once.

That is the problem this whole history is trying to solve.

It has been tried across every civilization we know of, in clay and fiber and silk and stone, long before silicon was a word and longer still before it was a chip. The people who tried it — the grandmothers and grandfathers of computing — were mostly not trying to build machines. They were trying to extend human possibility. To help people think more clearly, remember more faithfully, coordinate more fairly.

That is still what the best of this work is for.

## I. What Is a Tool to Think With?

A hammer extends the force of your arm. A telescope extends the reach of your eye. But what extends the reach of your *mind*?

The answer, it turns out, is very old: writing. The first tool to think with was not a machine but a mark in clay. When a Sumerian accountant in the city of Uruk pressed a reed stylus into a wet tablet around 3200 BCE and recorded that a certain storehouse held forty units of grain, something new entered the world: an idea that existed independently of any particular human brain.

The thought did not die when the accountant died. It waited in the clay.

This is so familiar to us now that we forget how revolutionary it was. Before writing, everything a community knew lived in human memory — in minds that aged, slept, forgot, and died. Knowledge passed through stories and songs, kept alive through constant repetition. Writing changed the relationship between the present and the past. It made human knowledge *persistent*.

Every computer program ever written, every database, every cloud server — all of it is an extension of that first act: pressing a reed into clay and saying, *let this thought outlast me*.

## II. The Eastern Roots: Zero, Algorithm, and the Abacus

The history of computing is usually told as a Western story, beginning in nineteenth-century England. This is incorrect, and the error matters.

The conceptual bedrock of all modern computing was built, in substantial part, in Asia and the Arab world.

**The zero came from India.**

This is worth pausing over, because without zero, modern mathematics — and therefore all computing — is impossible. You cannot do binary arithmetic (the system all computers use) without the concept of nothingness as a value.

Indian mathematicians — especially Brahmagupta, writing in the seventh century CE in Rajasthan — formalized zero as a number that could be added, subtracted, and reasoned about. They also developed the decimal positional system: the arrangement of digits by place value (ones, tens, hundreds) that we use today. Before this, you had to know hundreds of different Roman numeral symbols to write large numbers. After this, you needed only ten symbols and a sense of position.

The Arab scholar al-Khwārizmī carried this Indian system west in the ninth century, in a treatise called *Al-Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr waʾl-muqābala* — the "Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing." The word *algebra* comes from *al-jabr*, from his title. But another word comes from his name: *algorithm*. An algorithm is simply a step-by-step procedure for solving a problem. Al-Khwārizmī's name, Latinized by European scholars, became the word we now use for every recipe a computer follows.

Every time you use a navigation app that routes you through traffic, or a streaming service that recommends a film, or a search engine that retrieves a page — you are using an algorithm. You are using a method whose name carries a ninth-century Baghdad mathematician's name into the twenty-first century.

**The abacus came from across Asia.**

The *suanpan* — the Chinese abacus, with upper and lower beads on rods inside a wooden frame — was refined during the Han Dynasty (around 200 BCE to 200 CE) and spread throughout Asia. The Japanese *soroban*, the Russian *schoty*, and variations across the Islamic world all developed from similar principles: physical objects that held numerical state, so that the fingers and the frame could bear part of the cognitive load of calculation.

An abacus is not a calculator. It does not calculate. *You* calculate; the abacus remembers where you are. It is a memory extension — a way of offloading intermediate results from the brain to an object in the world, freeing your attention for the next step.

This is still what computers fundamentally are.

**The I Ching and binary code.**

In 1679, the German mathematician and philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed the binary number system: a way of representing any number using only two symbols, 0 and 1. He was apparently inspired, at least in part, by the ancient Chinese *I Ching* (Book of Changes), which had been using a binary notation for divination — broken and unbroken lines, combined into sixty-four hexagrams — for approximately three thousand years.

Leibniz saw in the I Ching's system something he had been working toward mathematically: a universal language of duality. Presence or absence. On or off. This is the language every modern computer speaks. The transistors inside your phone are simply very small switches, each in one of two states. Leibniz's binary system — and behind it, the I Ching's ancient grammar of broken and unbroken — is the alphabet of all digital computation.

## III. The Mechanical Imagination

The dream of a machine that could think — or at least calculate — is also very old.

**The Antikythera Mechanism.**

In 1901, divers salvaging a wreck off the Greek island of Antikythera pulled up a corroded lump of bronze. Over the following century, researchers slowly realized what it was: a hand-powered calculator, built around 100 BCE, capable of predicting the positions of the sun and moon, tracking the four-year Olympic cycle, and computing eclipses decades into the future.

It contained at least thirty interlocking bronze gears, machined to tolerances not matched in Europe for another fourteen centuries. It was operated by turning a single crank, and its outputs appeared in windows on its face like a mechanical almanac.

We do not know who built it. We do not know if it was unique or one of a kind among dozens. We know only that the desire to encode complex, recurring calculation into a physical object — to make the world's patterns legible through mechanism — is at least two thousand years old.

**The Jacquard Loom and the Programmable Machine.**

In 1804, a French silk weaver named Joseph Marie Jacquard invented an attachment for the mechanical loom that changed what "programming" could mean. Before Jacquard, a skilled weaver had to manually manipulate threads to produce complex patterns — an immensely skilled, slow, and expensive process. Jacquard's attachment used *punched cards*: stiff sheets with holes in precise positions. Where a hole appeared, a hook passed through and lifted a thread. Where there was no hole, the hook was blocked.

The pattern of holes was the program. Change the cards, change the pattern. The loom did not know what it was weaving. It followed instructions.

A century and a half later, the same punched card mechanism would be used to feed instructions into early computers. The weaving of silk patterns and the execution of mathematical programs are, from the machine's perspective, the same operation: reading a sequence of binary signals and responding accordingly.

Ada Lovelace — writing in 1843 about Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, which he was designing around the same punched-card principle — saw this equivalence and extended it further than anyone else of her time. She observed that if the Analytical Engine could manipulate numbers, it could, in principle, manipulate *any* symbol that could be encoded numerically. Notes, letters, musical pitches. "The Engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music," she wrote, more than a hundred years before anyone built a computer that could.

Lovelace also wrote what historians now recognize as the first algorithm intended for computation by a machine: a procedure for calculating Bernoulli numbers. She was twenty-seven years old. She called herself a practitioner of "poetical science" — the union of precise technical thinking with imaginative vision. She was the first to see that the machine was not merely a calculator but a symbol-manipulator of unlimited scope.

The machine Babbage designed was never built in his lifetime. But the concept — a programmable, general-purpose calculating engine, separating the method (program) from the mechanism (hardware) — is exactly what was reinvented a century later and called a computer.

## IV. What Is Information?

By the early twentieth century, humans had built telegraphs, telephones, and radios — machines that could transmit messages over long distances at the speed of electricity. But a strange question arose: what exactly were they transmitting?

Not the original voice. Not the paper the letter was written on. Something more abstract — a pattern that could be encoded, transmitted through a wire or through air, and decoded at the other end to reconstruct the original meaning.

In 1948, a mathematician at Bell Laboratories named Claude Shannon published a paper called "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" that answered this question with startling precision. Information, Shannon showed, is fundamentally a measure of *surprise*. A message tells you something only if it was not certain in advance. If someone always says "yes," their "yes" carries no information. If they sometimes say "no," every "yes" and "no" carries real information, because it resolves a genuine uncertainty.

This sounds like philosophy but it has practical consequences that govern every digital communication in existence. Shannon's theory explains how to compress data (remove the predictable parts), how much information a channel can carry (its *bandwidth*), and how to detect and correct errors in transmission (add strategic redundancy). Every time you stream a video, send a message, or store a file, Shannon's mathematics is governing what happens.

Shannon also showed that all information — in any medium, of any type — could be expressed in binary. Any message, encoded efficiently, reduces to a string of binary digits: bits. This is why "bit" (from *binary digit*) is the fundamental unit of digital information.

Shannon did not see this as a cold or mechanical insight. In an interview late in his life, he said what fascinated him most was the underlying unity it revealed: that sound, image, text, and mathematical symbol could all be understood as instances of the same thing. The diversity of human expression reduced, at the deepest level, to one simple question: what does this resolve?

## V. The Grandmothers and Grandfathers: What They Actually Believed

The people who built the conceptual foundations of modern computing in the mid-twentieth century are often portrayed as visionaries who simply saw what was coming and built it. This is partly true. But something more specific is also true: many of them had a particular *hope* about what computing would do for humanity, and that hope shaped what they built.

It is worth knowing what they hoped.

**Vannevar Bush** (1890–1974), the American engineer who directed American scientific research during World War II, published an essay in 1945 called "As We May Think." In it, he described a device he called the Memex: an imaginary desk that would store all of a person's books, records, and communications, and allow them to be retrieved and linked at high speed. Most importantly, it would allow the user to forge "trails" through knowledge — associative links between ideas, like the paths the human mind actually makes when thinking.

Bush was not excited about computation for its own sake. He was worried about something specific: the sum total of human knowledge was growing faster than any individual mind could navigate. Scientists were duplicating each other's work because they couldn't find what had already been done. Decisions were being made in ignorance of relevant knowledge that technically existed. The problem was not insufficient intelligence; it was insufficient *access*.

His Memex was designed to solve a problem of civilization: how do you make collective knowledge accessible to the individuals who need it? He was, in effect, imagining the World Wide Web, forty-five years before it was built.

**Norbert Wiener** (1894–1964) coined the word *cybernetics* — from the Greek *kubernetes*, meaning "helmsman" or "governor" — for the science of control and communication in both animals and machines. He showed that the mechanisms by which living systems regulate themselves (a body maintaining constant temperature, a community managing shared resources) and the mechanisms by which machines could be made to self-correct were instances of the same mathematical principle: *feedback*.

Wiener was also alarmed. He understood that machines which could regulate themselves could also be misused. In his 1950 book *The Human Use of Human Beings*, he argued that the purpose of computing should be human welfare and liberation — that automation should free people from mechanical drudgery, not concentrate power in fewer hands. "The machine," he wrote, "is the modern counterpart of the slave. Its existence puts the question of slavery in a new light."

He worried about this his whole life. He warned against the weaponization of cybernetics, against its use to concentrate economic or political control. His worry was not technophobia but exactly the opposite: he believed in what the technology could do for people, and feared what it would do *to* people if power over it was unequally distributed.

**J.C.R. Licklider** (1915–1990) was a psychologist who became director of computing research at the U.S. Advanced Research Projects Agency in the early 1960s — the office that eventually funded the ARPANET, the direct ancestor of the internet. In 1960, he published a paper called "Man-Computer Symbiosis."

The word *symbiosis* is biological: it names the relationship between two organisms that live together in mutual dependence, each providing something the other cannot supply on its own. A fig tree and the wasp that pollinates it. The mycorrhizal fungus and the forest. Each is diminished without the other.

Licklider proposed that humans and computers would eventually work in this way. Humans are good at setting goals, making intuitive judgments, recognizing relevance, and deciding what matters. Computers are good at exhaustive search, perfect memory, rapid calculation, and consistent execution. Neither, alone, is as capable as both together.

He imagined offices in which humans and computers would solve problems interactively — a partnership in real time. This was, in 1960, technically impossible: computers filled rooms, required hours to process instructions, and communicated only through punched cards. But Licklider had seen something real about the direction of development, and he spent his career funding the people who would make it real.

The internet exists, in large part, because Licklider believed in human-computer partnership and spent public money on the researchers who were building toward it.

**Douglas Engelbart** (1925–2013) read Licklider and asked: if computers are going to be tools to think with, they need to be *usable*. He spent his career designing not just what computers could compute but how humans could interact with them — what came to be called the "human-computer interface."

On December 9, 1968, Engelbart stood on a stage in San Francisco and demonstrated what became known as "The Mother of All Demos." In ninety minutes, he showed a live audience the computer mouse, hypertext links between documents, collaborative real-time text editing, and video conferencing. Every one of these technologies is now unremarkable. At the time, they did not exist outside his laboratory.

But what Engelbart was really demonstrating was a theory: that the most valuable use of computing was not automation (replacing human work) but *augmentation* (extending human capability). He called his framework "H-LAM/T" — the human being using Language, Artifacts, Methodology, and Training. His insight was that humans are not separate from their tools; they are the whole system, tool included.

And his deepest insight was recursive: the most powerful use of augmentation tools was to use them to improve the tools themselves. He called this "bootstrapping." The organization that uses its own coordination technology to improve its coordination technology is doing exactly what Engelbart identified as the highest-leverage activity.

**Grace Hopper** (1906–1992) and **Margaret Hamilton** (born 1936) built what the theorists imagined.

Hopper, a U.S. Navy rear admiral and pioneering computer scientist, invented the *compiler* — the program that translates human-readable instructions into the binary code computers actually execute. Before Hopper, you programmed in machine code: strings of ones and zeros. After Hopper, you could write instructions in something closer to English, and the computer would translate. She made programming human.

She also popularized the term "debugging": removing errors from software. The story goes that a moth was found trapped in a relay of the Harvard Mark II computer in 1947, causing a malfunction. Hopper's team taped it into the logbook with the note "First actual case of bug being found." Whether apocryphal or not, the word stuck. Every time a programmer "debugs" their code, Hopper is somewhere in the etymology.

Hamilton named *software engineering* during the Apollo program, when she led the team that wrote the software for the guidance computers on the spacecraft that landed humans on the moon. "Software engineering" was not yet a field. She invented it because the work required it: systems in which a software failure meant the loss of human life demanded the same rigor as mechanical engineering. Her software successfully handled a near-catastrophe during the Apollo 11 lunar landing — a radar switch caused the computer to be overloaded with data, and Hamilton's priority-scheduling system correctly abandoned lower-priority tasks to focus on the critical guidance functions that landed the spacecraft safely.

She was thirty-three years old.

## VI. The Network Dream

By the 1960s, Licklider's funding at ARPA had produced a community of researchers at universities across the United States who shared a belief in computing as a tool for human progress — and who were frustrated by the fact that their machines could not easily communicate with each other.

In 1969, a message was transmitted from a computer at UCLA to a computer at Stanford Research Institute. It was supposed to read "LOGIN." The receiving machine crashed after the first two letters. The first message sent over what would become the internet was "LO."

This was not a failure. It was the beginning of the most consequential communication infrastructure in history.

The design principles of the early ARPANET — and later the internet — were themselves a form of humanism. The network was built to be decentralized: there was no central node that could fail and take everything down. Traffic was broken into packets that could take different routes to reach the same destination. No single institution controlled what could be sent.

In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee — a British physicist working at CERN, the European particle physics laboratory in Switzerland — proposed a system for sharing documents over the internet using hypertext links. He called it the World Wide Web. He deliberately gave it away. He did not patent it. He did not build a company around it. He published the protocol openly so that anyone could build on it.

"This is for everyone," he said at the 2012 Olympics opening ceremony, from a stage in London, typing the words in real time on a NeXT computer, transmitted to screens around the stadium and around the world.

The open-source software movement grew from the same impulse: the belief that knowledge — including the knowledge of how to build software — should be a commons, not a commodity. Richard Stallman, who launched the GNU project in 1983 and articulated the "free software" philosophy, and Linus Torvalds, who in 1991 released the Linux operating system kernel as an open collaboration, were extending an argument that goes back to the Rochdale weavers and further back still: the tools of production should belong to the people who use and build them.

## VII. What They Actually Believed

If there is a thread running through all of this — from the Sumerian clay tablet to the Linux kernel — it is something like this:

*The problems that matter most to human civilization are problems of coordination and collective memory. Any tool that helps human minds reach further, hold more, and work together more faithfully is a tool in service of civilization.*

Vannevar Bush believed this when he imagined the Memex as a solution to the crisis of collective knowledge.

Norbert Wiener believed this when he warned that the same tools could also be used to concentrate power and diminish human agency — and insisted, insistently and publicly, that they must not be.

Licklider believed this when he argued for symbiosis rather than replacement, for partnership between human judgment and machine precision.

Engelbart believed this when he spent twenty years building a lab that the rest of his profession largely ignored, certain that the path to better computing was understanding how humans think and extending it rather than simulating it.

Hopper believed this when she invented a compiler so that programming would not be limited to the small group of people who could hold machine code in their heads.

Hamilton believed this when she named software engineering as a discipline worthy of the same rigor as the engineering that puts spacecraft into orbit.

Berners-Lee believed this when he gave the web away.

These were not naive people. They understood the risks. Wiener practically invented the warning literature around technological power concentration. Turing, who created the foundational theory of computation in 1936 and helped break German codes in World War II, spent his later years asking what it would mean for a machine to think — and proposing not an answer but a test, because he understood that the question touched something profound about consciousness and humanity that science could not yet reach.

They were optimistic about the potential of computing because they understood what problem it was for. Not for profit. Not for power. For the oldest problem: how do you build tools that help human beings think and coordinate and remember well enough to make a civilization worth living in?

## VIII. Where We Are

Most of this document has been about the past. The present is harder to assess clearly.

Computing has, in many ways, delivered on its early promise. The internet has made more information accessible to more people than any technology in history. Open-source software underlies most of the world's digital infrastructure. Languages that were spoken by few are now accessible through translation tools. Scientific research that would have taken decades can now be attempted in years.

And computing has also, in many ways, betrayed the early hope. The network that was designed to be decentralized has consolidated into a small number of very large platforms. The information abundance Vannevar Bush feared would overwhelm researchers has been joined by a deliberate information pollution that makes it hard to know what to trust. The automation Wiener warned would concentrate economic power has, in many sectors, done exactly that.

The early visionaries would not be entirely surprised. They worried about these things. Wiener spent the 1950s writing books about it.

What they would want, I think, is for us to keep asking the same question they were asking: *What is this for?* Not "what does this do?" but "what does this serve?" The technology is not the answer. It is only ever a means, shaped by the purposes of the people who build and govern it.

The grandmother who pressed clay to hold grain counts, the mathematician in Baghdad who named the procedure we call algorithm, the weaver in Lyons who encoded patterns in punched cards, the admiral who made code speak English, the engineer who imagined a desk that could hold all of human knowledge — they were all trying to solve the same problem. They were trying to build tools that extended what human beings could do together.

That problem is still open. It will always be open, because the complexity of the world always outpaces the tools we build to navigate it. The right response is not discouragement. It is what it has always been:

*Now, let us continue the work.*
