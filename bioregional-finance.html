Bioregional Finance

How communities can build economic infrastructure grounded in place — securing water, food, fiber, and housing for generations

Techne Institute / Spring 2026

I

Finance That Knows Where It Lives

Most financial systems are placeless. Capital flows toward the highest return regardless of geography, community, or ecology. A dollar invested in a bond fund might end up financing a soybean farm in Brazil, a data center in Virginia, or a strip mall in Phoenix. The investor never knows, and the system is designed so they never need to.

Bioregional finance starts from the opposite premise. It asks: what happens when economic relationships are grounded in the places where people actually live? When capital circulates within a community rather than extracting from it? When the financial instruments themselves are shaped by the ecology of a specific landscape?

A bioregion — from Greek bios (life) and Latin regio (territory, direction) — is a geographic area defined by ecological boundaries rather than political ones. A watershed, a climate zone, a soil type, a vegetation pattern. The Front Range of Colorado is a bioregion. The Northern California coast is a bioregion. The Great Lakes basin is a bioregion.

Bioregional finance is the practice of building economic infrastructure that follows these natural boundaries. It is not a theory waiting for implementation. It is happening now, in different forms, in different places, at different scales.

○
Place-Committed
Capital stays in the bioregion. Value circulates among the people who create it. Returns are measured in community resilience, not only financial yield.
∇
Ecologically Bounded
Economic boundaries follow ecological ones — watersheds, foodsheds, fibersheds. The landscape shapes the financial infrastructure, not the other way around.
Δ
Community Governed
The people who live in a place make the allocation decisions. Governance is local, democratic, and accountable to the community it serves.
II

A Landscape of Practice

Bioregional finance is not one model. It is a family of approaches that share a common grammar: value is created and circulated within a place-based community, verified against actual conditions in that place, and governed by the people who live there. Four examples illustrate the range.

Community Currencies

Sarafu Network
Kenya, expanding globally

Communities create mutual credit based on the real goods and services their members provide. A baker commits bread, a tutor commits lessons, a farmer commits produce. The currency represents these commitments and circulates among neighbors.

Works on basic feature phones. No cryptocurrency sophistication required. The 2025 survey shows measurable impact for low-income families.

Ecological Verification

Regen Network
Global registry

Credits issued when someone verifies that ecological conditions in a specific place actually improved — soil carbon increased, biodiversity grew, water quality changed. Not projections. Measurements.

The Biocultural Stewardship Methodology integrates Indigenous land stewardship into verification. Place and people are inseparable.

Regional Fiber Systems

Fibershed
Northern California, expanding

Everything needed to clothe a community — fiber-growing, processing, weaving, dyeing, sewing — sourced from within 150 miles. The supply chain becomes a local economy. Byproducts feed building material and food systems.

Founded by Rebecca Burgess from a personal challenge: one wardrobe, all local. Now a regional system that builds soil while producing textiles.

Coordinated Ecological Work

Swarm Ecosystems
Distributed, bioregion-linked

Bounties fund specific ecological coordination tasks — watershed monitoring, data infrastructure, knowledge commons. Participants claim work, deliver results, build shared resources that compound over time.

The mechanism evolves based on what actually works. Coordination patterns are tested in practice, not designed in theory.

What these systems share: none of them treat a bioregion as something to be represented in financial markets. Each one builds economic relationships that are embedded in a place, accountable to its people, and shaped by its ecology.

III

The Four Sheds

Everyone knows what a watershed is — the geographic area draining to a common point. But the "shed" is a more general concept: any geographic area defined by the flow of a specific resource. The same principle that organizes water governance can organize food, fiber, and housing.

These four sheds are the natural units of bioregional commons governance. The boundaries are drawn by ecology and use, not by administration.

Four Resource Sheds
∇
Watershed
aqua — water
The area draining to a common point. Streams, aquifers, snowpack, irrigation. The oldest and most legally developed shed concept. In the American West, water governance has shaped entire civilizations.
In Colorado, community ditch companies have governed water allocation cooperatively for over a century — quasi-cooperative structures predating modern cooperative law.
≡
Foodshed
panis — bread
The area providing food to a community. Farms, ranches, processing, distribution, markets. Where the food comes from, how it gets to you, and who grows it.
The Farmers Land Trust model: agricultural land held for diversified production of food, forage, fiber, medicine, and ecological stewardship. The land feeds the community in perpetuity.
∼
Fibershed
filum — thread
The landscape providing materials for textiles and building. Wool, hemp, flax, timber, straw. The fiber cycle: grow, harvest, process, manufacture, use, return to soil.
Fibershed demonstrated that a complete textile supply chain can operate within 150 miles — and that the same fiber-growing practices that produce cloth also build soil carbon.
□
Housing Shed
domus — home
The area within which people live, work, and raise families. Shelter is not a commodity — it is a condition for participation in community life. Housing costs determine who can stay.
The Kensington Corridor Trust in Philadelphia takes real estate off the market and holds it for collective community control. The neighborhood governs its own development.

The sheds overlap. The watershed feeds the foodshed. The foodshed produces fiber. Housing clusters where food and water are accessible. Bioregional finance holds the relationships between sheds as much as the individual resources — because a community needs all four to thrive.

IV

A Legal Structure for Permanence

How do you ensure that a community's essential resources — its water, food, fiber, and housing — remain available across generations, regardless of market pressures, political changes, or individual decisions?

The answer is a perpetual purpose trust — a legal structure that exists to serve a stated purpose, with no expiration date and no individual beneficiaries who can redirect its assets.

How It Works

An ordinary trust has beneficiaries — specific people who benefit from its assets. A purpose trust has no beneficiaries. It exists to serve a purpose, and an appointed enforcer holds the trustee accountable to that purpose. The trust cannot be sold, liquidated, or redirected. It serves its purpose in perpetuity.

This is what makes purpose trusts uniquely suited to holding commons. The trust does not serve shareholders, donors, or even members. It serves the stated purpose — and the stated purpose can be: to secure access to water, food, fiber, and housing within this bioregion for all who live here.

Precedents

Patagonia — In 2022, Yvon Chouinard transferred the company to a purpose trust. Voting rights stay with the trust (ensuring the mission persists), while economic rights flow to environmental causes. The company generates revenue and conducts commerce — but its purpose cannot be overridden by any future owner, because there are no future owners. Only the purpose endures.

Kensington Corridor Trust — In Philadelphia, KCT takes real estate off the market and places it in perpetual trust for collective community control. Board members include neighborhood residents and business owners. Properties are rehabilitated and reactivated to preserve affordability. The land can never be sold to the highest bidder.

Vesi — A startup now deploying the KCT model nationally, beginning in Trenton, NJ. The vision: acquire portfolios of 30-40 properties per neighborhood, maintaining long-term affordability and occupant equity while scaling across communities.

Why Not a Nonprofit?

Purpose trusts can engage in market-rate transactions and use revenue to cross-subsidize mission work. A 501(c)(3) depends on grants and donations. A community land trust has limited commercial flexibility. A purpose trust can own businesses, conduct commerce, and generate self-sustaining revenue — all while bound by fiduciary duty to its purpose rather than to shareholder returns.

V

Encoding Natural Law and Earth Law

Some things are older than any statute. The recognition that water is not a commodity. That soil feeds everyone who lives above it. That shelter is a precondition for participation in community life. That the thread binding a garment connects the wearer to the land where the fiber grew.

These recognitions are what legal scholars call natural law — principles that exist independent of legislation, rooted in the conditions of life itself. Earth law extends the frame: ecosystems have inherent rights, not merely instrumental value to humans.

A purpose trust is the mechanism for translating these recognitions into durable legal structure. It encodes what a community already knows into a form that persists across generations.

How the Encoding Works
Recognition
What a Community Knows
Water, food, fiber, and shelter are preconditions for life. Access to them is a right older than any statute. The land sustains the community; the community stewards the land.
Legal Theory
Natural Law and Earth Law
Procedural rights to establish, maintain, participate in, be informed about, and seek redress for ecological commons. The commons is not given by government — it is recognized by it. (Wijdekop, Earth Law Alliance)
Structure
The Perpetual Purpose Trust
Removes the resource from market alienability. Binds fiduciary duty to the purpose of securing the four sheds. Appoints enforcers with standing. Operates in perpetuity — outlasting any administration, market cycle, or individual intention.
Document
The Right to Commons
The trust instrument declares that access to water, food, fiber, and housing within a bioregion is a right the trust exists to secure. This is the constitutional document of the commons — binding across generations.

The U.S. Public Trust Doctrine already recognizes this principle for navigable waters — states must manage them for public benefit. A purpose trust extends the same recognition to the four sheds, in a structure that does not depend on state action. Where governments fail their public trust obligations, the purpose trust provides an alternative path.

The Financial Architecture

A purpose trust secures assets. But assets need active management — stewardship, maintenance, revenue generation, response to changing conditions. The emerging model for bioregional-scale finance integrates three functions into a single facility.

This framework — developed by Dark Matter Labs and the Buckminster Fuller Institute (2024) — is not another layer of financial intermediation. It is a structural change in who makes allocation decisions. Financial resource governance moves from distant capital markets to the bioregional community itself.

The problem bioregional finance addresses is not capital scarcity. It is capital direction. Thirty-five times more money flows toward ecological destruction than restoration. The existing financial architecture produces this ratio reliably. Changing the ratio requires changing the architecture — not asking the existing system to behave differently.

Building the Path

Start Where You Have Social Infrastructure

The legal structure grows around the social infrastructure, not the other way around. Where people already cooperate — in a ditch company, a food co-op, a neighborhood association, a fiber collective — the purpose trust formalizes what is already happening and makes it durable across generations.

A cooperative provides the operating layer. Members govern the organization democratically. Patronage accounting tracks each person's contribution and distributes surplus based on participation, not ownership. Venture reciprocity creates commons funding without extraction.

A purpose trust provides the durability layer. The cooperative manages the assets; the trust holds them in perpetuity. No future board, no market downturn, no political shift can redirect the assets away from the stated purpose.

Together, they compose into something neither can achieve alone: a self-governing economic institution that is both responsive (the cooperative adapts to changing conditions) and permanent (the trust endures across generations).

"The question is not whether this will happen. It is: what is the minimum viable shed? One watershed? One neighborhood? One food system?"

VIII

The Right to Commons

The progression from cooperative to purpose trust to bioregional financing facility answers the structural question: what institutions hold the commons? But there is a prior question: what does the commons actually provide, to whom, and on what terms?

The Right to Commons (RtC) is a modular economic agreement that answers this question. It is collectively underwritten, term-structured, and regionally priced. It secures access to basic human needs through mutual obligation rather than individual purchasing power.

The reframing is fundamental. Modern economies treat basic needs as individual purchasing problems. You need food, so you buy food. You need shelter, so you rent or mortgage shelter. Each transaction is bilateral, mediated by money, and priced by markets that are largely indifferent to whether you can actually afford the outcome. The RtC asks a different question: not how do we pay for basic needs? but how do we organize the provisioning of basic needs?

Eight Modules, Composed by Need

The agreement is modular by design. Each module covers a discrete domain of need, with its own provisioning standard, supply architecture, and contribution schedule. Participants compose their agreement from available modules based on need, capacity, and regional availability.

Food

Nutritional Security

Cooperative supply chains, CSA shares, community kitchens, food forests. Provisioning standard: caloric sufficiency, nutritional adequacy, cultural appropriateness.

Shelter

Housing Stability

Cooperative housing, community land trusts, managed rental pools. Highest capital intensity — anchors long-term agreements. Security of tenure, not just a roof.

Water

Clean Water Access

Municipal partnerships, well cooperatives, community filtration. In many regions, supplementary to public infrastructure — ensuring access for those excluded.

Energy

Basic Power

Community solar, cooperative utilities, micro-grids. Potentially self-funding or revenue-positive — surplus energy sold to the grid offsets provisioning costs.

Health

Primary Care

Cooperative clinics, mutual aid health funds, community health workers. Begin with preventive care, dental, vision, mental health — less regulatory friction.

Knowledge

Education and Skills

Libraries, apprenticeships, skill-sharing networks, digital learning. Provisioning standard: foundational literacy plus one domain-specific learning pathway.

Connectivity

Digital Infrastructure

Mesh networks, cooperative ISPs, device access, digital literacy. Prerequisite for participation in most other modules in a digital economy.

Care

Child and Elder Care

Cooperative care circles, time banking, shared-space arrangements. The module that most reveals the inadequacy of purely monetary contribution models.

Term Structure

Agreements are issued at defined terms, each with different pricing and commitment depth. The analogy to bond maturities is intentional: shorter terms carry higher per-unit cost because the collective bears more uncertainty. Longer terms reward commitment with stability — like a 30-year mortgage costing less per month than a 5-year note.

Deep Commitment

Four Channels of Contribution

The agreement is bilateral. Participants both contribute to and draw from provisioning capacity. Contribution flows through four channels:

$
Capital
Monetary contribution scaled to capacity. Progressive — those with more contribute more. The most legible channel, but not the most important.
◊
Labor
Direct participation in provisioning. Farming, building, cooking, repairing, teaching. The work that makes the modules function.
Δ
Stewardship
Governance participation, monitoring, knowledge sharing. The attention that keeps the commons legible and accountable. Ostrom's design principles require it.
○
Attention
Showing up. Bearing witness. Maintaining social fabric. The least quantifiable contribution — and possibly the most essential for the agreement's long-term viability.

Collective Underwriting

Who underwrites the agreement? The collective itself — a cooperative, a purpose trust, a federation. The underwriting entity pools contributions, manages provisioning contracts with suppliers and service providers, and maintains solidarity reserves against disruption.

The risk being underwritten is not default on a loan. It is the risk that a member's basic needs will go unmet. The agreement is secured not by collateral but by the productive capacity of the commons, the governance commitments of participants, and the relational bonds of community membership.

The Commons Provisioning Index

Each module is priced regionally based on the actual cost of provisioning in a specific place — accounting for cooperative infrastructure, non-monetary labor, and shared resources that conventional price indices ignore. This produces a Commons Provisioning Index: a bioregional metric that measures what it actually costs to secure basic needs through commons-based provisioning. Not a consumer price index. A commons capacity index.

The CPI enables cross-regional comparison — so that a Food module in Boulder can be meaningfully compared to one in Taipei or coastal Kenya. This matters for migration planning, development benchmarking, and solidarity transfers between regions with different provisioning capacities.

IX

The Invitation

Bioregional finance is not waiting for permission. Community currencies circulate in Kenya. Eco-credits verify ecological work on chain. Fiber systems operate within 150 miles. Cooperatives manage shared infrastructure in Boulder. Purpose trusts hold neighborhoods in Philadelphia.

And the Right to Commons gives these practices a shared grammar — a modular agreement structure that composes provisioning across domains, rewards long-term commitment, and makes the cost of securing basic needs legible at bioregional scale.

What remains to be built is the connective architecture — the legal and financial structure that holds these practices together across time, across resource sheds, across the generations who will inherit what we build or fail to build.

Open Questions

  1. What is the minimum viable "shed" for a purpose trust? The smallest functional unit might be a single watershed, a single neighborhood, or a single food system. Starting where social infrastructure already exists.
  2. How do cooperative governance and purpose trust structure compose legally? The cooperative operates; the trust holds. Can an LCA be the managing entity of a perpetual purpose trust? The legal composition is unexplored.
  3. Which module comes first — and for whom? Knowledge is the easiest to start (many cooperatives already provision it). Food is the most legible to explain. Energy may be self-funding. The choice reveals what you value.
  4. How are Indigenous sovereignty and treaty rights engaged? Any bioregional trust on the Front Range must engage with Arapaho, Cheyenne, and Ute historical claims. This is not a footnote. It is a foundational question.
  5. How does attention become accountable? The RtC contribution model includes "attention" — showing up, bearing witness, maintaining social fabric. How do you track this without destroying what makes it valuable?
  6. What is the tax treatment? Non-charitable purpose trusts are taxed differently from charities and nonprofits. The answer shapes the financial viability of the entire model.
  7. How does the "Right to Commons" agreement interface with existing property law? Colorado's prior appropriation doctrine privatizes water. The Public Trust Doctrine pushes back. A purpose trust navigates between them.

The word regio means the act of drawing a boundary — of saying "this place." Bioregional finance is the act of drawing economic boundaries that correspond to ecological ones. The Right to Commons is the agreement that lives within those boundaries — a mutual obligation, modular and term-structured, through which a community underwrites the conditions of a dignified life. Not charity. Not insurance. A commons.
